How to Check a Massage Therapist's License in Utah

By J Massage SLC · Updated September 2026

Every few weeks someone in a Salt Lake City forum asks a version of the same question: where can I find a legitimate, licensed massage therapist. Sometimes it is a car-accident client who needs real soft-tissue work and does not want to gamble. Sometimes it is someone who had a bad experience somewhere else and wants to know how to avoid a repeat. It is a fair question, and the good news is that Utah gives you a free public way to answer it in about two minutes.

Here is how the lookup works, what it does and does not tell you, and the other things worth screening before you hand over a card.

The two-minute license lookup

Utah licenses massage therapists through the Division of Professional Licensing, part of the Department of Commerce. Anyone can search the register for free at secure.utah.gov/llv, no account needed.

Type the therapist's first and last name, or search the business name if that is all you have. Then read three things on the result:

  • Profession. It should read Massage Therapist. Utah also issues massage apprentice and massage assistant credentials, which are legitimate but are not the same thing as a full license.
  • Status. Active is what you want. Expired means exactly what it says.
  • The name matches. Sounds obvious, and it is the step people skip. If the studio will not tell you which therapist you are booked with, that is information too.

The Division also publishes a disciplinary actions and citations search, so a licence that has been sanctioned is not hidden from you. If you would rather ask a person, the Division's number is (801) 530-6628.

The part most people do not know: establishments are registered too

Checking the individual therapist is the common advice. There is a second layer that almost nobody mentions.

Under Utah Code 58-47b-302.2, massage establishments in Utah must register with the Division of Professional Licensing, and the Division has opened that application process. That means the business itself is accountable to the state, not just the person with their hands on your back.

Practically, this gives you two independent things to check rather than one. A studio operating properly will have no trouble with either question, and will not be strange about you asking.

What the license does and does not tell you

A license is a floor, not a ceiling. It tells you the therapist met the state's training and examination requirements, passed a background check, and has not lost the right to practise. That is genuinely worth confirming, and it rules out the situations people are actually worried about.

What it does not tell you is whether they are any good for what you need. Licensing does not grade technique, and it does not tell you whether someone who is excellent at relaxation work is the right choice for a stubborn shoulder. That part comes from what they specialise in, how they run an intake, and how they handle the first ten minutes.

What else to screen before you book

Once the license checks out, a short list separates a professional studio from a coin flip.

  • Published pricing. Real rates on a real page, not "call for details." Ours are on the pricing page.
  • A named modality list. A studio that offers deep tissue, Swedish, sports, prenatal and the rest as distinct services has therapists trained in them, not one generic hour with a different label.
  • An intake step. Someone should ask what is going on with your body before the session, especially after a collision, a surgery, or during pregnancy.
  • Clear boundaries in writing. Draping policy, what to expect, what is not offered. Vagueness here is the single biggest warning sign in this industry.
  • Reviews that mention people by name. Specific reviews are harder to fake than generic five-star lines. Ours are on the reviews page.

Green flags in the first ten minutes

You can tell a great deal before any real work starts. A therapist who asks what you do all day, where you feel it, and whether anything is off limits is building a session for you. A therapist who checks pressure early and then again once they are into the deeper work is paying attention. Proper draping, without you having to ask, is standard practice and not a courtesy.

The other green flag is willingness to say no. If you ask for deep pressure on an area that should not take it that week, a good therapist tells you and offers something better. That answer is worth more than an hour of agreeable silence.

Red flags, and what to do about them

Trust the obvious ones. A studio that will not name your therapist, will not quote a price, or gets evasive about draping is telling you something. So is a session where the pressure never gets checked, or where you feel you cannot speak up.

If something happens during a session that crosses a line, you can end it. Say stop, and a professional therapist stops immediately, no explanation owed. Afterward you can file a complaint with the Division of Professional Licensing, which is what the disciplinary record is built from. If you believe a crime occurred, contact the police. Reporting is also how the next person avoids the same experience, which is the part people tend to underweight when they decide it is not worth the trouble.

Special cases worth naming

Two situations come up often enough in local threads to deserve their own note.

After a car accident. Soft-tissue work after a collision should start with what your doctor has said and what imaging showed. A licensed therapist will ask about that and will work around anything acute. Massage of neck musculature is not the same as cervical manipulation, which is a chiropractic technique, and conflating the two is behind a lot of the fear people carry into a first appointment.

During pregnancy. Prenatal work is its own training, with side-lying positioning and areas that are avoided. It is not a regular massage with a pillow. We wrote a full guide to prenatal massage in the third trimester if you are weighing it, and the short version is that you want someone who does it regularly and who will tell you to check with your provider first.
