Sore After a Massage: What's Normal, What Isn't, and How to Ask for the Right Pressure

By J Massage SLC · Updated September 2026

A version of this question turns up in massage forums almost every week. Someone gets a deep tissue session, wakes up the next morning barely able to stand, and writes in asking whether that is normal, whether they are allowed to say something about it, and what they should do now.

It is a good question and the answer has three parts: what ordinary soreness is, what it is not, and how to keep the whole thing from happening again. The third part is the one nobody covers, and it is the one that actually matters, because almost every bad outcome we hear about traces back to a conversation that did not happen in the room.

What normal post-massage soreness feels like

Ordinary soreness after a massage is dull, diffuse and tender. It feels like the day after a workout you had not done in a while. You notice it when you move into the area, it is spread across a general region rather than pinpointed, and it does not stop you doing what you normally do.

On timing: it typically shows up within a few hours, peaks inside the first 24, then eases. Most people are back to normal within two to three days. The direction matters more than the number. Soreness that improves each day is behaving the way it should. Soreness that is worse on day two than it was on day one is telling you something different.

It is more likely after a first session, after a long gap between massages, after deeper work than you are used to, and after a session that covered a lot of ground rather than one area. None of that is a problem. It is your body responding to work it has not had recently.

What is not normal

These are the ones worth paying attention to:

  • Sharp, specific pain rather than a spread-out ache, especially if you can put one finger on it.
  • Pain that limits you. Not being able to stand, bend, or get through an ordinary day is past the range of expected soreness.
  • Pain that is getting worse after the first 24 hours instead of settling.
  • Pain somewhere the therapist barely worked. This one confuses people, and it is worth flagging rather than explaining away.
  • Numbness, tingling, or weakness anywhere, which is a different category from soreness entirely.
  • Significant swelling, or unusually dark urine. These are rare, and they are same-day medical questions rather than massage questions.

We are a massage studio, not a medical provider, so the honest version is this: if any of the above describes you, the useful next call is to your doctor, not to a forum and not to us. That is not us dodging the question. It is that nobody can assess an injury through a screen, and the people telling you confidently that it is just fascia releasing cannot either.

Worth separating two things that get conflated constantly: working the muscles of the neck is not the same as cervical manipulation, which is a chiropractic technique involving a quick thrust at a joint. A lot of fear people carry into a massage belongs to the other thing.

Deep tissue does not mean heavy-handed

Here is the misunderstanding underneath most of these stories. Deep tissue massage describes which layer of muscle and connective tissue gets worked. It does not describe how much force is applied.

That distinction gets lost, and it gets lost in both directions. Clients book deep tissue expecting an ordeal and then brace through the entire hour. Therapists, wanting to deliver what was asked for, push harder than the tissue wants. Both of those produce a worse massage than the same hour done well, and the second one produces the mornings that start these forum threads.

If firm hand pressure tends to feel sharp on you, ask about Ashiatsu. The broad sole of the foot spreads deep pressure over a wider area.

Why bracing makes the work less effective

A muscle under pressure it does not want responds by guarding. It contracts to protect itself. So the harder someone pushes past your limit, the more the tissue resists, and the less of the session goes where it was supposed to go.

That is the practical argument against no pain, no gain, and it is more persuasive than the safety one. Pressure past your edge is not brave. It is just less effective, and you paid for the hour either way.

The workable range is the edge of comfortable: firm enough that you feel it clearly, light enough that you are breathing normally and your body is not fighting it. If you are gripping the table or holding your breath, you have gone past the useful part.

How to ask for what you want, in the room

Your therapist cannot feel what you feel. They are reading your breathing, your muscle tone and whatever you tell them, and the first two are much less reliable than the third.

Things that work:

  • Give a direction, not a verdict. Lighter here. A bit more across the shoulder. That is good, hold there. Easier to act on than good or fine.
  • Answer the check-in honestly. When a therapist asks about pressure, that is a real question. Fine when it is not fine is the most common way people end up sore.
  • Say where, at the start. What has been bothering you, how long, what makes it worse, and anything a doctor has said. A session built around that is a different hour from a general pass.
  • Mention the desk. If you cannot sit for twenty minutes without needing to lie down, say that specifically, because the work for it is usually hips and mid-back as much as the spot between your shoulder blades where you feel it.
  • Say stop if you need to. At any point, for any reason. A professional stops immediately.

One more, for the people who book a massage every few months and get told each time that they do not stretch enough: it is worth saying out loud what you already do. Someone training six days a week with a real mobility routine is a different case from someone who has not stretched since school, and the session should be built differently. Tight is not automatically a discipline problem.

What to do in the first two days

If you are ordinarily sore, this is most of it. Drink water. Keep moving gently rather than going completely still, because staying still tends to stiffen things further. Use heat or cold based on which one actually feels better on you, since people differ and there is no prize for picking the theoretically correct one. Skip the hard workout that day. A warm bath or the infrared sauna add-on helps some people and does nothing for others.

And if you were planning your massage around training, the timing matters more than most people expect. Our guide to sports massage before or after a workout covers that specifically.

Tell the studio, even if it feels awkward

People sit on this, because it feels like making a complaint about someone who was perfectly nice to them. Reframe it. You are giving the studio information they need and currently do not have.

A well-run studio notes pressure preferences in your file so the next session starts in the right place rather than guessing again. And if a session went genuinely wrong, they need to know that too, for reasons that have nothing to do with you. Staying quiet protects nobody.

Common questions

How long should you be sore after a massage?

Ordinary post-massage soreness usually peaks within the first 24 hours and eases over the following day, so most people feel normal again inside 48 to 72 hours. It should feel like the dull ache after a good workout, it should improve each day, and it should not stop you doing everyday things. Soreness that is still severe after three days, or that is getting worse rather than better, is worth a call to your provider.

Is it normal to be in pain after a deep tissue massage?

Mild next-day tenderness is normal, especially in the first few sessions or after a long gap. Sharp pain, pain that limits your movement, or pain in a place the therapist barely worked is not the expected outcome. Deep tissue refers to the layer of muscle being worked, not to how hard someone leans on you, and a session should never be something you have to breathe through.

Should a massage hurt to be effective?

No. Pressure that makes you tense up works against the session, because a guarded muscle resists the work rather than releasing. Effective pressure sits at the edge of comfortable, where you can still breathe normally and stay relaxed. If you are gripping the table or holding your breath, the pressure is past useful and into counterproductive.

What should I do if I am sore after a massage?

Drink water, move gently rather than resting completely still, and use heat or cold depending on which feels better to you. Avoid a hard workout the same day. If pain is severe, is getting worse after the first day, comes with significant swelling, or comes with unusually dark urine, treat that as a same-day medical question rather than a massage question.

Can I tell my massage therapist the pressure is wrong?

Yes, and you should, during the session rather than after it. A therapist cannot feel what you feel, and most people say nothing and then leave unhappy. Saying lighter here, or that is good but not deeper, is the single most useful thing you can do in the room. A professional therapist wants that information and will adjust immediately.

Should I tell the studio if a massage left me in pain?

Yes. It is not a complaint so much as information the studio needs, and a good one will want to hear it. It lets them note the pressure in your file so the next session starts differently, and it tells them something about how that session was run.
